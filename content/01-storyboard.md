### Spread 02 — Electric cars are wonderful

**Prose (left):**
> Electric cars are wonderful.
>
> They run on electricity instead of petrol, so they help clean up the air and slow down
> climate change. Every year there are more of them on the road.
>
> This is very good news.
>
> But it comes with a small catch. The catch is not the car. The catch is the timing.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a cheerful electric car plugged in at a tidy suburban home at dusk, leaf/clean motif.]`

---

### Spread 03 — Everybody charges at the same time

**Prose (left):**
> Here is the catch.
>
> People drive home from work in the evening, and they plug in their cars at about the same
> time, around six o'clock. Then dinner, then television, then bed.
>
> So a great many cars all start drinking electricity at once, right when homes are already
> using the most. This is called the **evening peak**.
>
> The grid finds the evening peak quite stressful.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a row of houses at 6 PM, many cars all plugging in simultaneously; a rising red "peak" curve hovering over the rooftops.]`

---

### Spread 04 — A simple idea, and two people who studied it

**Prose (left):**
> There is a simple idea that helps. If the cars do not all need to be full until morning,
> why not let some of them charge later, quietly, in the middle of the night when nobody
> else wants the electricity?
>
> This idea is called **smart charging**.
>
> Two people at George Washington University spent four years studying it: Pingfan, a PhD
> student, and John, his advisor. Unusually, John found Pingfan first and sent him an email.
> Their first chat was meant to last one hour. It lasted three.
>
> They had three big questions.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: friendly two-person portrait of Pingfan and John (from provided photos) at a table covered in charts; warm, collegial. NOTE: character likeness, may need /ph-anime.]`

## PART 1 — STUDY 1: WILL PEOPLE SAY YES?

### Spread 05 — The first question

**Prose (left):**
> The first question was the most human one.
>
> Smart charging only works if people agree to it. Would a car owner really let the
> electricity company decide *when* their car charges overnight?
>
> There were two flavours to ask about. In the gentler one, **Supplier-Managed Charging**,
> the company just chooses the timing, but promises your car is full by morning. In the
> bolder one, **Vehicle-to-Grid**, your car also sends power *back* to the grid when it is
> needed, like a little battery on wheels.
>
> One asks for patience. The other asks for a favour.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: split scene. Left, a car charging calmly overnight (SMC). Right, a car with arrows flowing back out to the neighborhood (V2G).]`

---

### Spread 06 — Ask the right people

**Prose (left):**
> Now, who do you ask?
>
> Many earlier studies asked the general public, most of whom had never owned an electric
> car and had never charged one. That is a bit like asking people about a country they have
> never visited.
>
> So Pingfan and John did something better. They found **1,356 real electric-car owners**
> and asked *them*. To keep out pretenders, the survey hid which cars were electric among
> seventeen hundred others. You could not fake your way in.
>
> These were people who actually knew what charging is like.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a friendly crowd of varied real car owners standing beside their EVs; a subtle "1,356" motif.]`

---

### Spread 07 — How to ask without asking

**Prose (left):**
> You cannot simply ask "how much do you value flexibility?" People do not think in numbers
> like that.
>
> Instead they were shown pairs of pretend programs and asked to pick: this one, that one,
> or "no thanks." Each program had different knobs turned up or down: a bit of cash now, a
> few dollars a month, the freedom to override, a promise about how full the battery would be.
>
> Make people choose, over and over, and the maths quietly reveals what they truly care about.
>
> This is called a **choice experiment**.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: two side-by-side "program cards" with sliders/dials, and a hand choosing between them plus a "no thanks" option.]`

---

### Spread 08 — What the gentle program needs

**Prose (left):**
> For the gentle program, Supplier-Managed Charging, the answer was a pleasant surprise.
>
> People did not mainly want money. They wanted **flexibility**: a guarantee that the car
> would be charged enough by morning, and the freedom to override on a night they needed it.
>
> In fact, offering good flexibility and *no money at all* was enough to get about **half**
> of owners to say yes. A small steady payment, a couple of dollars a month, nudged it
> higher still.
>
> People will share control. They just want to be sure they will not be stranded.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a reassured owner asleep while the car charges; a soft "guaranteed by 7 AM" gauge filling up.]`

---

### Spread 09 — What the bolder program needs

**Prose (left):**
> The bolder program, Vehicle-to-Grid, was a different story.
>
> Sending power back out of your own car is a bigger ask, and people wanted to be paid for
> it. Here, money did most of the talking. Pay generously per event, and many owners were
> happy to take part.
>
> Two programs, two quite different bargains: patience can be bought with a promise, but a
> favour wants a fee.
>
> To help utilities find the right mix, the team also built a little web tool where you can
> turn the knobs yourself and watch how many people would sign up.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a car exporting power to a glowing neighborhood with coins/credits flowing back to the owner; a small dashboard/slider tool inset.]`

## PART 2 — STUDY 2: DOES IT HELP, AND DOES IT PAY?

### Spread 10 — Two new questions

**Prose (left):**
> So people will say yes. Lovely. But that only raised two harder questions.
>
> First, does smart charging *actually* bring the evening peak down by a meaningful amount?
>
> Second, and this was the real heart of the second study: **does the money make sense?**
> Utilities have to pay people to take part. Is the grid benefit worth the bill?
>
> Good intentions are not the same as a good deal. Pingfan set out to check.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a balance scale, peak-reduction (a flattened curve) on one pan, a stack of dollars on the other.]`

---

### Spread 11 — Borrow the real answers

**Prose (left):**
> Here is the clever part. Instead of guessing how many people would join, the second study
> **borrowed the real answers** from the first one.
>
> Those 1,356 honest opinions became a curve: pay this much, and roughly this many people
> sign up.
>
> Then the team built a computer model of an entire region's electricity and let the cars
> charge inside it, night after night, across a whole year.
>
> They did this for two very different places: sunny **California** and chilly **New York**.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a map split between California (sunny) and New York (snowy), with a survey curve feeding into a glowing grid simulation.]`

---

### Spread 12 — Moving the mountain

**Prose (left):**
> The model did one main thing: it took the charging that used to happen at the evening
> peak and slid it gently into the quiet **valley** after midnight.
>
> When enough cars did this, the evening mountain shrank.
>
> In California, on a good day, smart charging shaved up to **5.6 gigawatts** off the peak,
> enough power for millions of homes. On an average day it trimmed about a tenth of the peak.
> New York shaved less, because it is a smaller, flatter place, but it still helped.
>
> The idea works. Now, the money.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a before/after load curve. A tall evening peak on the left flattening, with the load moving into a gentle overnight valley.]`

---

### Spread 13 — Why the bill grows so fast

**Prose (left):**
> To get people to join, the utility pays everyone the same monthly thank-you.
>
> Here is the trouble. The easy-to-please sign up cheaply. To tempt the *next* person, you
> must raise the payment, and you have to raise it for **everyone**, including all the people
> who would have joined for less.
>
> So the closer you push toward "everybody," the more wildly the bill climbs. The last few
> holdouts are spectacularly expensive guests.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a staircase of enrolling people where each new step costs dramatically more; coins piling up steeply on the right.]`

---

### Spread 14 — The surprise: more is not better

**Prose (left):**
> This led to the second study's favourite finding.
>
> You do not want everybody. You want **enough**.
>
> Signing up around **a third to a half** of owners captured almost all of the peak benefit,
> for a small fraction of the cost. Chasing full participation paid more and more for less
> and less.
>
> And there was a twist. Push *too* many cars into the same midnight valley and they pile up
> there instead, building a brand-new peak in the dark. On about one in seven California
> days, trying too hard simply moved the mountain rather than flattening it.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: the overnight valley with too many cars crowding in, forming a new small peak at midnight; a wry "oops" mood.]`

---

### Spread 15 — Place and season matter

**Prose (left):**
> Where you are matters too.
>
> Sunny California has a deep, reliable valley every night of the year, because its solar
> panels rest in the evening, so smart charging pays off steadily in every season.
>
> Chilly New York is trickier. On winter nights, everyone's heating keeps the grid busy and
> half-fills the valley already, leaving less room to tuck the cars into. So the same idea
> earns its keep in summer but struggles in the cold.
>
> The lesson: smart charging is not one-size-fits-all. Tune it to the place.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: two nighttime scenes side by side. California: a wide-open valley with room for cars. New York winter: a valley already crowded by glowing heated homes.]`

---

## PART 3 — STUDY 3: THE TOOL THEY HAD TO BUILD

### Spread 16 — A problem behind the research

**Prose (left):**
> Now, rewind for a moment.
>
> To ask 1,356 people all those careful questions, you need a survey. And when the team went
> looking for a tool to build one, every option annoyed them.
>
> The polished ones were expensive. The free ones were limited. And almost none let you save
> your survey as something you could share, check, and run again exactly the same way later.
>
> For researchers, that last part is a real problem. So they did the obvious thing. They
> built their own.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a frustrated researcher facing clunky/locked survey tools (a price tag, a padlock), with a lightbulb forming.]`

---

### Spread 17 — Meet surveydown

**Prose (left):**
> They called it **surveydown**.
>
> Most survey tools make you click around in a website to build your questions. surveydown
> lets you simply *write* your survey as plain text, the same way you would write a document,
> and it turns that text into a real, working web survey.
>
> It is **free**, it is **open-source**, and anyone can use it.
>
> What began as a tool for one lab quietly became a tool for everyone.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a simple text file on one side transforming into a clean, live web survey on the other; friendly "free & open" badge.]`

---

### Spread 18 — Why plain text is secretly wonderful

**Prose (left):**
> Writing a survey as plain text sounds dull. It is secretly wonderful.
>
> Because it is just text, you can keep a perfect history of every change, hand it to a
> colleague, and reproduce the exact survey years later. No locked files, no lost work.
>
> And because real code runs underneath, the survey can be clever: skipping questions that
> do not apply, reacting to earlier answers, shuffling things at random.
>
> Best of all, you keep your own data. It is yours, on your terms.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a tidy version-history timeline of a survey, branching question logic shown as a friendly flowchart; a small "your data, your house" motif.]`

---

### Spread 19 — A gift to other researchers

**Prose (left):**
> surveydown was built for Pingfan and John's own work, but it did not stay there.
>
> It now lives on the public software shelf where anyone with a little R can install it for
> free. Researchers in all sorts of fields, public health, economics, education, and more,
> have picked it up, filed suggestions, and even taught it to speak six languages.
>
> Thousands of downloads later, a frustration had turned into a small gift to the wider
> research community.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a globe with researchers in different settings all using the same tool; subtle stars/downloads counters, multi-language hellos.]`

## PART 4 — BRINGING IT TOGETHER

### Spread 20 — Three questions, one story

**Prose (left):**
> Step back, and the three studies are really one story told in three parts.
>
> The **first** asked people: *will you take part, and on what terms?*
>
> The **second** asked the grid and the wallet: *does it help, and is it worth it?*
>
> The **third** built the open tool that made asking so many people possible, and handed it
> to everyone else.
>
> Preferences, then proof, then the means to keep asking.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a triptych: a crowd (people), a grid/curve (system), a survey tool (method), linked by a single thread.]`

---

### Spread 21 — What it all means

**Prose (left):**
> So, what should the world take from four years of this?
>
> Smart charging genuinely works. It can shave the evening peak and help the grid welcome
> more electric cars without panic.
>
> But it pays off best **in moderation**, and **tuned to the place**: aim for enough people,
> not all of them, and respect the seasons and the local grid.
>
> And the tools we use to learn such things should be open, so the next person can build on
> the last.

`[IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a calm evening city of the near future, electric cars charging staggered through the night, the grid steady and bright.]`
